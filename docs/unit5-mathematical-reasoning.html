2. Types of Reasoning

Reasoning is the process of using information, facts, rules or relationships to arrive at a conclusion.

Important types include:

  • Deductive reasoning
  • Inductive reasoning
  • Analogical reasoning

3. Deductive Reasoning

Deductive reasoning moves from a general statement or rule to a specific conclusion.

Structure

General Rule
     ↓
Specific Case
     ↓
Conclusion

Example

All teachers are professionals.

Ravi is a teacher.

Therefore:

Ravi is a professional.

The conclusion follows from the premises.

Key Point

Deduction = General → Specific

4. Inductive Reasoning

Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations to a general conclusion.

Structure

Specific Observations
        ↓
     Pattern
        ↓
General Conclusion

Example

A researcher observes that:

  • Student A performs better after regular practice.
  • Student B performs better after regular practice.
  • Student C performs better after regular practice.

The researcher may conclude:

Regular practice may improve student performance.

The conclusion is based on observations and is not necessarily certain.

Key Point

Induction = Specific → General

5. Analogical Reasoning

Analogical reasoning identifies a relationship between two things and applies a similar relationship to another pair.

Example

Teacher : School :: Doctor : Hospital

The relationship is:

A teacher works in a school.

Therefore:

A doctor works in a hospital.

Key Point

Analogy = Relationship between two pairs

6. Deductive vs Inductive Reasoning

Deductive Inductive
General → Specific Specific → General
Starts with a rule/principle Starts with observations
Conclusion can follow necessarily from true premises Conclusion is usually probable
Example: All A are B; X is A; therefore X is B Several observed A are B; therefore A may generally be B

7. Number Series

A number series is a sequence of numbers arranged according to a particular pattern.

Example

2, 4, 6, 8, 10, ?

The difference between consecutive numbers is:

+2, +2, +2, +2

Therefore:

Answer = 12

8. Common Number Series Patterns

When solving a number series, check for:

  1. Addition
  2. Subtraction
  3. Multiplication
  4. Division
  5. Squares
  6. Cubes
  7. Alternating patterns
  8. Increasing differences
  9. Decreasing differences
  10. Combination of operations

9. Addition Series

Example:

5, 10, 15, 20, ?

Pattern:

+5, +5, +5

Therefore:

Answer = 25

10. Subtraction Series

Example:

50, 45, 40, 35, ?

Pattern:

-5, -5, -5

Therefore:

Answer = 30

11. Multiplication Series

Example:

2, 4, 8, 16, ?

Pattern:

×2, ×2, ×2

Therefore:

Answer = 32

12. Division Series

Example:

64, 32, 16, 8, ?

Pattern:

÷2, ÷2, ÷2

Therefore:

Answer = 4

13. Square Number Series

Example:

1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ?

These are squares:

1² = 1
2² = 4
3² = 9
4² = 16
5² = 25

Therefore:

6² = 36

14. Cube Number Series

Example:

1, 8, 27, 64, ?

These are cubes:

1³ = 1
2³ = 8
3³ = 27
4³ = 64

Therefore:

5³ = 125

15. Increasing Difference Series

Example:

2, 5, 10, 17, 26, ?

Find the differences:

+3, +5, +7, +9

The differences increase by 2.

Next difference:

+11

Therefore:

26 + 11 = 37

16. Alternating Series

Sometimes odd and even positions follow different patterns.

Example:

2, 5, 4, 10, 6, 15, ?

Separate the positions:

Odd positions:
2, 4, 6, ?

Even positions:
5, 10, 15

Odd positions increase by 2.

Therefore:

Answer = 8

17. Strategy for Number Series

When you see a number series:

Step 1

Check differences.

Step 2

Check multiplication or division.

Step 3

Check squares and cubes.

Step 4

Check increasing or decreasing differences.

Step 5

Check odd and even positions separately.

Step 6

Look for a combination of operations.

18. Letter Series

A letter series consists of letters arranged according to a particular pattern.

To solve letter series questions, remember the alphabetical positions:

A = 1
B = 2
C = 3
D = 4
E = 5
...
Z = 26

19. Simple Letter Series

Example:

A, C, E, G, ?

Positions:

1, 3, 5, 7, ?

The pattern is:

+2

Therefore:

I = 9

Answer:

I

20. Reverse Alphabet

Remember:

Z = 26
Y = 25
X = 24
...
A = 1

Example:

Z, X, V, T, ?

The positions are:

26, 24, 22, 20

Pattern:

-2

Therefore:

18 = R

Answer:

R

21. Letter Series with Skipping

Example:

A, D, G, J, ?

Positions:

1, 4, 7, 10

Pattern:

+3

Next position:

13 = M

Answer:

M

22. Codes

Coding-decoding questions involve replacing letters, numbers or words according to a specific rule.

Example

If:

CAT → DBU

Each letter is shifted by +1:

C → D
A → B
T → U

Therefore:

DOG → EPH

  • Backward shifting
  • Reversal
  • Substitution
  • Numerical coding
  • Symbol coding
  • Combination of operations

24. Relationships

Relationship questions test the ability to identify relationships between people, objects or concepts.

Examples include:

  • Blood relationships
  • Family relationships
  • Direction relationships
  • Logical relationships

25. Blood Relationship Problems

Example:

A is the brother of B. B is the sister of C. How is A related to C?

A is male and is the sibling of B.

B is the sibling of C.

Therefore:

A is the brother of C.

Strategy

Draw a small relationship diagram instead of trying to remember everything mentally.

27. Fractions

A fraction represents a part of a whole.

It is written as:

a ÷ b

where:

  • a = numerator
  • b = denominator

and:

b ≠ 0

Example

3/5

means 3 parts out of 5 equal parts.

28. Types of Fractions

Proper Fraction

Numerator < Denominator.

Example:

3/5

Improper Fraction

Numerator ≥ Denominator.

Example:

7/5

Mixed Fraction

Contains a whole number and a fraction.

Example:

2 1/3

29. Addition of Fractions

Example:

1/2 + 1/3

LCM of 2 and 3 = 6.

Therefore:

1/2 = 3/6

and:

1/3 = 2/6

Therefore:

(3 ÷ 6)+(2 ÷ 6)
=
(5 ÷ 6)

30. Multiplication of Fractions

Example:

2/3 × 4/5

Multiply numerators:

2 × 4 = 8

Multiply denominators:

3 × 5 = 15

Therefore:

(2 ÷ 3) × (4 ÷ 5)
=
(8 ÷ 15)

31. Time and Distance

Important formula:

Speed = Distance ÷ Time

Therefore:

Distance = Speed × Time

and:

Time = Distance ÷ Speed

Easy Triangle

       Distance
      ----------
      Speed | Time

32. Example – Speed

A car travels 120 km in 3 hours.

Find its speed.

Speed = Distance ÷ Time
=(120 ÷ 3)
=40 km/h

Answer:

40 km/h

33. Unit Conversion

Important conversions:

1 km = 1000 m

1 hour = 60 minutes

1 minute = 60 seconds

For speed:

1 km/hour = 5/18 m/second

1 m/second = 18/5 km/hour

34. Average Speed

Average speed is:

Average Speed=
(Total Distance ÷ Total Time)

It is not always equal to the simple average of two speeds.

Example

If a person travels:

  • 60 km in 2 hours
  • 40 km in 1 hour

Total distance:

60+40=100 km

Total time:

2+1=3 hours

Therefore:

Average Speed=(100 ÷ 3)
=33.33 km/h

35. Ratio

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind.

It is written as:

a : b

Example

If a class has 20 boys and 30 girls:

Ratio of boys to girls:

20:30

Divide by 10:

2:3

36. Equivalent Ratios

Ratios can be multiplied or divided by the same non-zero number.

Example:

2:3

Multiplying both terms by 4:

8:12

Therefore:

2 : 3 = 8 : 12

37. Proportion

A proportion states that two ratios are equal.

Example:

2/3 = 4/6

Therefore:

2 : 3 = 4 : 6

This is a proportion.

38. Direct Proportion

In direct proportion, when one quantity increases, the other also increases in the same relationship.

Example:

If 1 book costs ₹100:

2 books = ₹200
3 books = ₹300

More books → More cost.

39. Inverse Proportion

In inverse proportion, when one quantity increases, the other decreases.

Example:

If more workers are employed to complete the same task, the required time may decrease, assuming other conditions remain the same.

Workers ↑
    ↓
Time ↓

40. Percentage

Percentage means “per hundred”.

The symbol is:

%

Formula

Percentage=
(Part ÷ Whole) × 100

41. Example – Percentage

A student scores 72 marks out of 90.

Percentage:

(72 ÷ 90) × 100
=80%

Answer:

80%

42. Converting Percentage to Fraction

Example:

25%

means:

25/100

Simplify:

1/4

Therefore:

25% = 1/4

43. Percentage Increase

Formula:

Percentage Increase=
(Increase ÷ Original Value) × 100

Example

A price increases from ₹500 to ₹600.

Increase:

600-500=100

Percentage increase:

(100 ÷ 500) × 100 = 20%

44. Percentage Decrease

Formula:

Percentage Decrease=
(Decrease ÷ Original Value) × 100

Example

A price decreases from ₹800 to ₹600.

Decrease:

800-600=200

Percentage decrease:

(200 ÷ 800) × 100 = 25%

45. Profit and Loss

Important terms:

Cost Price (CP)

The price at which an item is purchased.

Selling Price (SP)

The price at which an item is sold.

Profit

When:

SP > CP

Profit:

Profit = SP − CP

Loss

When:

CP > SP

Loss:

Loss = CP − SP

46. Profit Percentage

Profit%=
(Profit ÷ CP) × 100

Example

CP = ₹500

SP = ₹600

Profit:

600-500=100

Profit percentage:

(100 ÷ 500) × 100 = 20%

47. Loss Percentage

Loss%=
(Loss ÷ CP) × 100

Example

CP = ₹800

SP = ₹600

Loss:

800-600=200

Loss percentage:

(200 ÷ 800) × 100 = 25%

48. Discount

Discount is the reduction given on the marked price.

Important Terms

Marked Price (MP) = Price written on the product.

Discount = Reduction from marked price.

Selling Price (SP) = Price paid after discount.

Formula:

Discount = MP − SP

49. Discount Percentage

Discount%=
(Discount ÷ MP) × 100

Example

Marked price = ₹1000

Discount = ₹200

Therefore:

Discount%=
(200 ÷ 1000) × 100
=20%

Selling price:

1000-200=₹800

50. Simple Interest

Simple Interest is calculated only on the original principal.

Formula:

Simple Interest = (P × R × T) ÷ 100

where:

  • P = Principal
  • R = Rate of interest
  • T = Time

Example

Find the simple interest on ₹5,000 at 10% per year for 2 years.

SI=(5000 × 10 × 2 ÷ 100)
=₹1000

Amount:

Amount = Principal + Interest
=5000+1000
=₹6000

51. Compound Interest

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus accumulated interest.

For annual compounding:

A = P × (1 + R/100)^T

Compound interest:

CI = A − P

where:

  • A = Amount
  • P = Principal
  • R = Rate
  • T = Time

Example

For ₹1,000 at 10% per year for 2 years:

A = 1000 × (1.10)^2
A=1210

Therefore:

CI = 1210 − 1000
=₹210

52. Simple Interest vs Compound Interest

Simple Interest Compound Interest
Calculated on original principal Calculated on accumulated amount
Interest remains based on principal Interest earns further interest
Formula: PRT/100 Uses compound growth formula

53. Averages

Average is a measure of central tendency.

Formula:

Average=
(Sum of observations ÷ Number of observations)

54. Example – Average

Find the average of:

10, 20, 30, 40, 50

Sum:

10+20+30+40+50=150

Number of observations:

5

Therefore:

Average = 150 ÷ 5 = 30

Answer:

30

55. Finding the Missing Value Using Average

Suppose the average of five numbers is 20.

Therefore:

Total = 20 × 5
=100

If four numbers are:

10, 15, 20, 25

Their sum is:

70

Missing number:

100-70=30

Answer:

30

For UGC NET aptitude questions:

Step 1

Read the question carefully.

Step 2

Identify what is given.

Step 3

Identify what must be found.

Step 4

Write the relevant formula.

Step 5

Substitute the values.

Step 6

Calculate carefully.

Step 7

Check whether the answer is reasonable.

58. Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 – Using the Wrong Base for Percentage

Profit percentage is calculated using Cost Price, not Selling Price.

Mistake 2 – Confusing Marked Price and Cost Price

Discount is calculated from the Marked Price.

Mistake 3 – Confusing Distance and Speed

Remember:

Speed = Distance ÷ Time

Mistake 4 – Forgetting Unit Conversion

For example:

Hours → Minutes
Kilometres → Metres

Mistake 5 – Incorrect Average

Average is:

(Total ÷ Number)

not simply the middle value.

62. Final Exam Tip

For numerical questions, do not immediately start calculating.

First ask:

What type of problem is this?

For example:

Question about price reduction
          ↓
       Discount

Question about buying and selling
          ↓
     Profit / Loss

Question about movement
          ↓
    Speed / Distance / Time

Question about comparison
          ↓
        Ratio

Question about "per hundred"
          ↓
      Percentage

Question about central value
          ↓
        Average

Correctly identifying the type of problem is often the first and most important step toward solving it.
