Unit II – Research Aptitude

1. Research

1.1 Meaning of Research

Research is a systematic and organized process of investigating a problem or question to discover new knowledge, verify existing knowledge, or develop a better understanding of a phenomenon.

The word research can be understood as:

Re + Search = Search again

Research involves systematic investigation rather than simply collecting information.

Example

Suppose a teacher wants to find out:

“Does online learning improve the programming performance of MSc AI students?”

The researcher may:

  1. Identify the research problem.
  2. Review existing literature.
  3. Formulate objectives.
  4. Select a research method.
  5. Collect data.
  6. Analyze the data.
  7. Interpret the results.
  8. Draw conclusions.
  9. Report the findings.

2. Characteristics of Research

Important characteristics of research include:

2.1 Systematic

Research follows an organized sequence of steps.

Problem
   ↓
Literature Review
   ↓
Objectives
   ↓
Methodology
   ↓
Data Collection
   ↓
Analysis
   ↓
Conclusion

2.2 Objective

Research should attempt to minimize personal bias.

The researcher should base conclusions on evidence.

2.3 Empirical

Research often relies on observations, measurements or collected data.

2.4 Logical

Research should follow logical reasoning.

The conclusions should be related to the evidence obtained.

2.5 Controlled

Where appropriate, researchers control relevant conditions so that the effect of particular variables can be studied.

2.6 Replicable

Research should be described sufficiently clearly so that other researchers can understand and, where appropriate, repeat the study.

2.7 Analytical

Research involves examining and interpreting information systematically.

2.8 Problem-Oriented

Research generally begins with a question, problem or area requiring investigation.

3. Types of Research

Research can be classified in different ways.

Some common classifications include:

  • Basic and applied research
  • Qualitative and quantitative research
  • Experimental and non-experimental research
  • Descriptive research
  • Historical research

The UGC NET syllabus specifically emphasizes:

  • Experimental
  • Descriptive
  • Historical
  • Qualitative
  • Quantitative research

4. Positivism

4.1 Meaning

Positivism is a philosophical approach that emphasizes the importance of observable, measurable and empirical evidence in the development of knowledge.

In a positivistic approach, researchers generally emphasize:

  • Observation
  • Measurement
  • Evidence
  • Objectivity
  • Scientific methods
  • Quantitative analysis

Example

Suppose a researcher wants to study whether a particular teaching method improves examination scores.

The researcher may:

  1. Select students.
  2. Apply the teaching method.
  3. Measure examination scores.
  4. Analyze the numerical results.
  5. Draw conclusions based on the evidence.

This approach emphasizes measurable outcomes.

5. Post-Positivism

Post-positivism developed partly as a response to the limitations of strict positivism.

It accepts the importance of empirical evidence but recognizes that knowledge may be incomplete and that researchers cannot always achieve perfect objectivity.

Important Ideas

Post-positivistic research recognizes:

  • Human knowledge can be imperfect.
  • Observations may contain limitations.
  • Researchers may have biases.
  • Findings should be supported by evidence.
  • Multiple methods may sometimes be useful.
  • Conclusions are often tentative rather than absolute.

Simple Difference

Positivism Post-positivism
Strong emphasis on objective observation Recognizes limitations of observation
Emphasizes measurable evidence Emphasizes evidence but recognizes uncertainty
Researcher aims for objectivity Researcher acknowledges possible bias
Often associated with quantitative approaches Can accommodate multiple approaches

Easy Memory Trick

Positivism → Observable and measurable evidence

Post-positivism → Evidence + limitations + uncertainty

6. Methods of Research

The syllabus identifies five major research methods:

  1. Experimental
  2. Descriptive
  3. Historical
  4. Qualitative
  5. Quantitative

7. Experimental Research

Experimental research is used to study cause-and-effect relationships by manipulating one or more variables and observing their effects.

Basic Structure

Independent Variable
        ↓
   Manipulation
        ↓
Dependent Variable
        ↓
     Result

Example

A researcher wants to determine whether a new teaching method improves student performance.

The researcher may have:

  • Experimental group
  • Control group

The experimental group receives the new teaching method, while the control group receives the existing method.

The performance of the groups is then compared.

Important Terms

Independent Variable

The variable that is manipulated or changed by the researcher.

Dependent Variable

The outcome that is measured.

Control Group

A group used as a comparison.

Experimental Group

The group receiving the experimental treatment or intervention.

Advantages

  • Useful for studying causal relationships.
  • Allows systematic control of variables.
  • Results can be measured quantitatively.

Limitations

  • Some experiments may be difficult or unethical to conduct.
  • Artificial experimental conditions may not completely represent real-world situations.

8. Descriptive Research

Descriptive research focuses on describing the characteristics of a population, situation or phenomenon.

It generally answers questions such as:

  • What?
  • Who?
  • Where?
  • When?
  • How much?

Example

A researcher wants to study:

“What percentage of college students use online learning platforms?”

The researcher may collect information through a survey and describe the findings.

Common Techniques

  • Surveys
  • Questionnaires
  • Observations
  • Interviews
  • Case studies

Key Point

Descriptive research describes what exists.

It does not necessarily establish that one variable causes another.

9. Historical Research

Historical research involves the systematic study of past events, developments, documents and records.

The researcher examines available evidence to understand and interpret historical developments.

Sources

Historical research may use:

  • Books
  • Government records
  • Official documents
  • Letters
  • Diaries
  • Newspapers
  • Archives
  • Institutional records
  • Photographs
  • Other historical documents

Example

A researcher may study:

“The development of higher education in India after independence.”

The researcher may examine historical documents, reports and records.

Key Point

Historical research focuses on the past.

10. Qualitative Research

Qualitative research focuses on understanding experiences, meanings, perceptions, opinions and social phenomena.

It generally deals with non-numerical information.

Examples of Data

  • Interview responses
  • Observations
  • Documents
  • Narratives
  • Field notes
  • Open-ended responses

Example

A researcher wants to understand:

“How do students experience online learning?”

The researcher may conduct interviews with students and analyze their responses.

Characteristics

  • Focuses on meaning and experience.
  • Often uses open-ended questions.
  • Provides detailed information.
  • Context is important.
  • Researcher plays an important role in data collection and interpretation.

Common Methods

  • Interviews
  • Observation
  • Focus groups
  • Case studies
  • Document analysis

11. Quantitative Research

Quantitative research focuses on numerical data, measurement and statistical analysis.

Examples

  • Examination scores
  • Age
  • Income
  • Number of students
  • Attendance percentage
  • Test scores

Example

A researcher studies:

“Is there a relationship between study time and examination marks?”

The researcher may collect:

  • Hours of study
  • Examination marks

and use statistical techniques to analyze the relationship.

Characteristics

  • Uses numerical data.
  • Uses measurement.
  • Often uses statistical analysis.
  • Can involve larger samples.
  • Emphasizes measurable variables.

12. Qualitative vs Quantitative Research

Qualitative Quantitative
Focuses on meanings and experiences Focuses on measurement
Mainly non-numerical data Numerical data
Interviews and observations are common Surveys and structured measurements are common
Open-ended questions Structured questions
Thematic interpretation may be used Statistical analysis is commonly used
Often explores “why” and “how” Often examines “how much”, “how many” or relationships

Easy Memory Trick

Qualitative
     ↓
Quality / Meaning / Experience

Quantitative
     ↓
Quantity / Numbers / Measurement

13. Steps of Research

Research normally follows a logical sequence.

Step 1 – Identify the Research Problem

The researcher identifies an issue or question that requires investigation.

Example

Does the use of AI-based learning tools improve student performance?

Step 2 – Review of Literature

The researcher studies existing research related to the problem.

Sources may include:

  • Research papers
  • Books
  • Journals
  • Conference papers
  • Theses
  • Reports
  • Reliable digital resources

Purpose

Literature review helps the researcher:

  • Understand existing knowledge.
  • Identify research gaps.
  • Avoid unnecessary duplication.
  • Develop the research framework.

14. Formulation of Research Objectives

Research objectives describe what the researcher intends to achieve.

Example

A study may have objectives such as:

  1. To identify the factors affecting student performance.
  2. To develop an AI-based learning system.
  3. To evaluate its effectiveness.

Objectives should be clear and related to the research problem.

15. Research Questions

Research questions specify what the researcher wants to investigate.

Example:

Does the use of an AI-based learning system improve examination performance?

Research questions help guide the study.

16. Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable statement or tentative proposition about a relationship between variables.

Example

Students using an AI-based learning system will achieve higher examination scores than students using conventional learning methods.

The hypothesis can be tested using appropriate data and statistical methods.

17. Research Design

Research design refers to the overall plan or framework used to conduct the research.

It specifies how the researcher will:

  • Collect data
  • Select participants
  • Measure variables
  • Analyze data

The design should be appropriate for the research problem.

18. Sampling

Sampling involves selecting a portion of a population for study.

Population

The entire group of interest.

Sample

A smaller group selected from the population.

Example:

If all MSc AI students in a university constitute the population, 100 selected students may constitute the sample.

Types of Sampling

Common approaches include:

  • Probability sampling
  • Non-probability sampling

19. Data Collection

Data may be collected using:

  • Questionnaires
  • Interviews
  • Observations
  • Experiments
  • Tests
  • Existing records
  • Documents

The method should be appropriate to the research objectives.

20. Data Analysis

Collected data must be analyzed systematically.

For quantitative research, analysis may involve:

  • Descriptive statistics
  • Correlation
  • Regression
  • Hypothesis testing

For qualitative research, analysis may involve:

  • Coding
  • Categorization
  • Theme identification
  • Interpretation

21. Interpretation of Results

The researcher explains what the findings mean in relation to:

  • Research questions
  • Objectives
  • Hypotheses
  • Existing literature

The researcher should distinguish between what the data actually demonstrate and what may only be inferred.

22. Conclusion

The conclusion summarizes the major findings of the research.

A good conclusion should be related to the research objectives and findings.

23. Research Report

The final research findings are communicated through a research report, thesis, dissertation, article, conference paper or other appropriate scholarly format.

24. Thesis Writing

A thesis is a detailed scholarly document presenting the research conducted by a researcher.

A typical thesis may contain:

  1. Title
  2. Abstract
  3. Introduction
  4. Review of Literature
  5. Research Methodology
  6. Results
  7. Discussion
  8. Conclusion
  9. References
  10. Appendices

The exact structure can vary according to institutional requirements.

25. Article Writing

A research article presents the findings of a research study in a scholarly publication.

A common structure is:

Title
  ↓
Abstract
  ↓
Introduction
  ↓
Literature Review
  ↓
Methodology
  ↓
Results
  ↓
Discussion
  ↓
Conclusion
  ↓
References

Important Sections

Title

The title should clearly indicate the subject of the research.

Abstract

The abstract provides a brief summary of:

  • Research problem
  • Method
  • Major findings
  • Conclusion

Introduction

Introduces the research problem and its background.

Methodology

Explains how the research was conducted.

Results

Presents the findings.

Discussion

Explains and interprets the findings.

Conclusion

Summarizes the major conclusions.

References

Lists the sources cited in the article.

26. Referencing

Referencing means acknowledging the sources used in research.

A researcher should provide references for ideas, information, data or statements obtained from other sources.

Why is Referencing Important?

Referencing:

  • Gives credit to original authors.
  • Helps readers locate sources.
  • Supports academic credibility.
  • Helps avoid plagiarism.

27. Referencing Styles

Different academic disciplines use different referencing styles.

Examples include:

  • APA
  • MLA
  • Chicago
  • IEEE
  • Harvard

APA

APA is widely used in social sciences and related disciplines.

IEEE

IEEE is commonly used in engineering and computer science publications.

The researcher should follow the referencing style required by the institution, journal or publisher.

28. Application of ICT in Research

ICT means:

Information and Communication Technology

ICT has become an important part of modern research.

28.1 Literature Search

Researchers can use digital resources to locate scholarly literature.

Examples include:

  • Online journal databases
  • Digital libraries
  • Institutional repositories
  • Search engines for scholarly literature

28.2 Data Collection

ICT can support data collection through:

  • Online questionnaires
  • Web forms
  • Online interviews
  • Digital surveys
  • Sensors
  • Mobile applications

28.3 Data Analysis

Researchers can use software for data analysis.

Examples include:

  • R
  • Python
  • SPSS
  • MATLAB
  • Excel

Different tools are appropriate for different types of research and analysis.

28.4 Reference Management

Reference-management software can help researchers organize citations and bibliographies.

Examples include:

  • Zotero
  • Mendeley
  • EndNote

28.5 Communication of Research

Researchers can communicate findings through:

  • Email
  • Video conferencing
  • Online conferences
  • Research repositories
  • Digital journals
  • Academic websites

28.6 Research Collaboration

ICT allows researchers in different geographical locations to collaborate using:

  • Cloud platforms
  • Video conferencing
  • Collaborative documents
  • Version control systems
  • Online repositories

29. Research Ethics

Research ethics refers to the principles and standards that guide responsible conduct of research.

Ethical research protects participants, maintains research integrity and promotes trust in scholarly work.

30. Informed Consent

Participants should receive appropriate information about the research and voluntarily agree to participate.

Participants should understand relevant aspects of participation before providing consent.

31. Confidentiality

Researchers should protect confidential information provided by participants.

Personal information should not be unnecessarily disclosed.

32. Privacy

Researchers should respect participants’ privacy.

Personal data should be collected and used appropriately.

33. Avoiding Harm

Researchers should take reasonable steps to avoid unnecessary:

  • Physical harm
  • Psychological harm
  • Social harm
  • Financial or other foreseeable harm

to participants.

34. Honesty in Research

Researchers should report their work honestly.

They should not:

  • Fabricate data.
  • Falsify results.
  • Deliberately misrepresent findings.

35. Fabrication

Fabrication means making up data or results that were never actually obtained.

Example

A researcher did not conduct an experiment but creates imaginary experimental results and presents them as real.

36. Falsification

Falsification involves manipulating research materials, procedures or data so that the research record or results are inaccurately represented.

Easy Difference

Fabrication = Making up data

Falsification = Manipulating data or research information

37. Plagiarism

Plagiarism involves presenting another person’s work, ideas or words as one’s own without appropriate acknowledgment.

Examples include:

  • Copying text without citation.
  • Using another person’s ideas without acknowledgment.
  • Presenting someone else’s work as original.

Avoiding Plagiarism

Researchers should:

  • Cite sources.
  • Use quotation marks for direct quotations where appropriate.
  • Paraphrase properly.
  • Provide references.

38. Authorship Ethics

Authorship should appropriately reflect meaningful scholarly contribution.

Researchers should avoid:

  • Giving authorship without meaningful contribution.
  • Excluding individuals who made qualifying contributions.
  • Misrepresenting contributions.

39. Conflict of Interest

A conflict of interest may arise when personal, financial or professional interests could affect—or reasonably appear to affect—the research process.

Researchers should disclose relevant conflicts according to applicable policies.

40. Research Integrity

Research integrity means conducting research responsibly and honestly.

It includes:

  • Honesty
  • Transparency
  • Accuracy
  • Accountability
  • Proper citation
  • Responsible data management
  • Ethical treatment of participants

Strong focus on objectivity Recognizes possible researcher bias
Often emphasizes measurement Recognizes uncertainty in knowledge
Scientific approach Modified scientific approach

Experimental vs Descriptive Research

Experimental Descriptive
Studies cause and effect Describes characteristics
Manipulation may occur No experimental manipulation is necessarily required
Variables may be controlled Existing conditions may be described
Often uses experimental groups Often uses surveys or observations

Qualitative vs Quantitative

Qualitative Quantitative
Meaning and experience Measurement and quantity
Non-numerical information Numerical information
Interviews, observations Surveys, measurements
Interpretation Statistical analysis
Often explores “why” and “how” Often examines “how much” and relationships

Fabrication vs Falsification vs Plagiarism

Term Meaning

Falsification Manipulating research/data or research records
Plagiarism Using others’ work without proper acknowledgment

  7. Historical research studies past events and records.

  9. Quantitative research focuses on numerical data and measurement.
