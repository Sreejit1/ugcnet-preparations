Unit VI – Logical Reasoning

1. Introduction

Logical Reasoning is the study of how we use statements, arguments, evidence and rules to reach conclusions.

In UGC NET, this unit mainly tests your ability to:

  • Understand arguments
  • Identify premises and conclusions
  • Understand categorical propositions
  • Identify Mood and Figure
  • Distinguish deductive and inductive reasoning
  • Identify formal and informal fallacies
  • Understand the use of language
  • Understand connotation and denotation
  • Use the Classical Square of Opposition
  • Solve analogy questions
  • Use Venn diagrams to test arguments
  • Understand Indian Logic
  • Understand the six Pramanas
  • Understand Anumana or inference
  • Understand Vyapti
  • Identify Hetvabhasas or fallacies of inference

2. What is Logic?

Logic is the systematic study of correct reasoning.

Simple meaning

Logic helps us answer:

“Does the conclusion really follow from the information given?”

Example

All humans are mortal.

Ravi is a human.

Therefore, Ravi is mortal.

Here:

  • “All humans are mortal” → Premise
  • “Ravi is a human” → Premise
  • “Ravi is mortal” → Conclusion

The conclusion follows logically from the premises.

3. Argument

An argument is a group of statements in which one or more statements provide support for another statement.

An argument normally contains:

  1. Premises
  2. Conclusion

Premise

A premise is a statement that provides evidence or support.

Conclusion

A conclusion is the statement that the argument tries to establish.

Example

All teachers are educated.

Anita is a teacher.

Therefore, Anita is educated.

Here:

Premise 1 → All teachers are educated.

Premise 2 → Anita is a teacher.

Conclusion → Anita is educated.

4. How to Identify the Conclusion

Look for conclusion indicators such as:

  • Therefore
  • Hence
  • Thus
  • So
  • Consequently
  • It follows that
  • Hence we can conclude

Example

Ravi studies regularly. Therefore, Ravi is likely to perform well.

The conclusion is:

Ravi is likely to perform well.

5. How to Identify Premises

Common premise indicators include:

  • Because
  • Since
  • As
  • Given that
  • For
  • Due to the fact that

Example

Ravi may perform well because he studies regularly.

Premise:

Ravi studies regularly.

Conclusion:

Ravi may perform well.

6. Structure of an Argument

An argument can be represented as:

Premise 1
Premise 2
Premise 3
   ↓
Conclusion

Example

All researchers use evidence.

Dr. Arun is a researcher.

Therefore:

Dr. Arun uses evidence.

7. Argument Forms

An argument form describes the logical structure of an argument.

Consider:

If P, then Q.

P.

Therefore Q.

This is a valid argument form called:

Modus Ponens

Example

If it rains, the ground becomes wet.

It is raining.

Therefore, the ground is wet.

8. Modus Ponens

Structure:

If P → Q
P
Therefore Q

Example

If a student studies, the student may improve.

Ravi studies.

Therefore, Ravi may improve.

Key Point

P → Q
P
Therefore Q

9. Modus Tollens

Structure:

If P → Q
Not Q
Therefore Not P

Example

If the computer is connected to the internet, the browser can access online content.

The browser cannot access online content.

Therefore, the computer may not be connected to the internet.

Key Point

P → Q
Not Q
Therefore Not P

10. Categorical Proposition

A categorical proposition is a proposition that relates two classes or categories.

Example

All students are learners.

It relates:

  • Students
  • Learners

A categorical proposition generally contains:

Quantifier + Subject + Copula + Predicate

Example:

All + students + are + learners

11. Four Types of Categorical Propositions

The four standard categorical propositions are:

Type Form Meaning
A All S are P Universal Affirmative
E No S are P Universal Negative
I Some S are P Particular Affirmative
O Some S are not P Particular Negative

Remember:

A → Universal Affirmative
E → Universal Negative
I → Particular Affirmative
O → Particular Negative

12. A Proposition – Universal Affirmative

Form:

All S are P.

Example:

All teachers are professionals.

Meaning:

Every member of the subject class belongs to the predicate class.

Symbolic form

All S are P

13. E Proposition – Universal Negative

Form:

No S are P.

Example:

No birds are mammals.

Meaning:

The two classes do not overlap.

No S are P

14. I Proposition – Particular Affirmative

Form:

Some S are P.

Example:

Some students are researchers.

Meaning:

At least one member of S belongs to P.

Some S are P

15. O Proposition – Particular Negative

Form:

Some S are not P.

Example:

Some students are not researchers.

Meaning:

At least one member of S does not belong to P.

Some S are not P

16. Easy Way to Remember A, E, I and O

A → All S are P

E → No S are P

I → Some S are P

O → Some S are not P

Memory Trick

A → All
E → No
I → Some + positive
O → Some + negative

17. Subject and Predicate

Consider:

All teachers are professionals.

Subject:

Teachers

Predicate:

Professionals

The proposition tells us something about the relationship between these two classes.

18. Distribution of Terms

Distribution tells us whether a proposition refers to all members of a class or only some members.

For UGC NET, remember this table:

Proposition Subject Predicate
A – All S are P Distributed Undistributed
E – No S are P Distributed Distributed
I – Some S are P Undistributed Undistributed
O – Some S are not P Undistributed Distributed

Easy memory

A → S distributed
E → Both distributed
I → Neither distributed
O → P distributed

19. Mood of a Categorical Syllogism

Mood refers to the combination of the types of the three propositions in a categorical syllogism.

The three propositions are:

  1. Major premise
  2. Minor premise
  3. Conclusion

Example

All M are P       → A
All S are M       → A
Therefore,
All S are P       → A

The mood is:

AAA

20. Figure of a Categorical Syllogism

Figure depends on the position of the middle term in the premises.

There are four figures.

Figure 1

M – P
S – M
-----
S – P

Figure 2

P – M
S – M
-----
S – P

Figure 3

M – P
M – S
-----
S – P

Figure 4

P – M
M – S
-----
S – P

Important

There are:

4 Figures

21. Mood and Figure

Mood and Figure together identify the form of a categorical syllogism.

Example:

AAA – Figure 1

The mood tells us:

What type of propositions are used?

The figure tells us:

Where is the middle term located?

22. Deductive Reasoning

Deductive reasoning moves from a general statement to a specific conclusion.

General
   ↓
Specific

Example:

All humans are mortal.

Ravi is a human.

Therefore Ravi is mortal.

If the premises are true and the argument is valid, the conclusion follows necessarily.

23. Inductive Reasoning

Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations to a general conclusion.

Specific observations
        ↓
General conclusion

Example:

Student A improved after practice.

Student B improved after practice.

Student C improved after practice.

Therefore, regular practice may improve performance.

The conclusion is probable rather than necessarily certain.

24. Deductive vs Inductive Reasoning

Deductive Inductive
General → Specific Specific → General
Based on rules or premises Based on observations
Conclusion can be necessary Conclusion is usually probable
Validity is important Strength is important
Example: syllogism Example: generalization

25. Formal Fallacy

A formal fallacy is an error in the logical structure or form of an argument.

Example

If P → Q

Q

Therefore P

This is not a valid form.

This error is called:

Affirming the consequent.

26. Informal Fallacy

An informal fallacy is an error caused by the content, language, assumptions or context of an argument rather than simply its logical structure.

Examples include:

  • Ad hominem
  • Appeal to authority
  • Appeal to emotion
  • Straw man
  • Hasty generalization
  • False cause
  • Appeal to ignorance

27. Ad Hominem

Ad hominem means attacking the person instead of addressing the argument.

Example

Ravi’s argument about education is wrong because Ravi is inexperienced.

The person’s characteristics are attacked instead of the argument.

Key Point

Attack the person
instead of
Answering the argument

28. Hasty Generalization

A conclusion is made about a whole group based on insufficient examples.

Example

A student meets two rude tourists from a particular city and concludes:

Everyone from that city is rude.

This is a hasty generalization.

29. Appeal to Authority

An argument claims something is true simply because an authority or famous person says it.

Example

A famous actor says this medicine works, so it must work.

The statement requires appropriate evidence, not merely celebrity endorsement.

30. Straw Man Fallacy

A person’s argument is changed into a weaker or exaggerated version and then attacked.

Example

Person A:

We should reduce unnecessary use of plastic.

Person B:

Person A wants to completely ban every plastic product.

Person B has changed the original argument.

This is a straw man.

31. False Cause

False cause occurs when one event is assumed to have caused another without sufficient evidence.

Example

I wore a particular shirt and passed the examination. Therefore, the shirt caused my success.

The conclusion does not establish causation.

32. Uses of Language

Language can be used for different purposes.

Important uses include:

1. Informative

Provides information.

Example:

The examination begins at 10 AM.

2. Expressive

Expresses feelings or emotions.

Example:

I am extremely happy today.

3. Directive

Attempts to influence behaviour.

Example:

Please close the door.

4. Performative

An utterance performs an action in an appropriate context.

Example:

I apologize.

33. Denotation

Denotation refers to the direct or literal meaning of a term.

Example:

“Rose” denotes a particular type of flowering plant.

It refers to the object or class represented by the word.

34. Connotation

Connotation refers to the associated, implied or emotional meaning of a term.

For example, the word:

“Home”

may connote:

  • Comfort
  • Security
  • Family
  • Belonging

Easy difference

Denotation → Direct meaning

Connotation → Associated meaning

35. Denotation vs Connotation

Denotation Connotation
Literal meaning Associated meaning
Direct reference Implied/emotional association
More objective Can vary with context
Example: snake = reptile Snake may connote danger or fear

36. Classical Square of Opposition

The Classical Square of Opposition shows relationships among the four categorical propositions:

A – All S are P
E – No S are P
I – Some S are P
O – Some S are not P

The basic structure is:

        A
       / \
      /   \
     E     I
      \   /
       \ /
        O

More precisely, the traditional square contains four important relationships:

  1. Contradictory
  2. Contrary
  3. Subcontrary
  4. Subalternation

37. Contradictory Propositions

Contradictory propositions cannot both be true and cannot both be false.

Pairs:

A ↔ O

E ↔ I

Examples:

A:

All students are punctual.

O:

Some students are not punctual.

If A is true, O must be false.

If O is true, A must be false.

38. Contrary Propositions

Contrary propositions cannot both be true, but they can both be false.

Pair:

A ↔ E

Example:

A:

All students are punctual.

E:

No students are punctual.

Both cannot be true simultaneously.

But both can be false if:

Some students are punctual and some are not.

39. Subcontrary Propositions

Subcontrary propositions can both be true, but traditionally cannot both be false.

Pair:

I ↔ O

Example:

I:

Some students are punctual.

O:

Some students are not punctual.

Both can be true.

40. Subalternation

Subalternation is the relationship between:

A → I

E → O

A is the universal affirmative and I is the particular affirmative.

E is the universal negative and O is the particular negative.

Traditional rule

If the universal proposition is true, the corresponding particular proposition is true.

41. Square of Opposition – Quick Revision

Relationship Pair

Contradictory E – I
Contrary A – E

Subalternation E – O

42. Analogical Reasoning

Analogy is based on similarity or relationship.

Example:

Teacher : School :: Doctor : Hospital

Relationship:

Teacher works in School.

Doctor works in Hospital.

Therefore, the relationship is preserved.

43. How to Solve Analogy Questions

Follow these steps:

Step 1

Identify the relationship between the first pair.

Step 2

Apply the same relationship to the second pair.

Step 3

Select the option that maintains the same relationship.

Example

Bird : Nest :: Bee : ?

Answer:

Hive

Because:

Bird → Nest

Bee → Hive

44. Venn Diagrams

A Venn diagram uses circles to represent relationships between classes or sets.

It is useful for:

  • Understanding categorical propositions
  • Testing arguments
  • Showing inclusion
  • Showing exclusion
  • Showing overlap

45. Venn Diagram – All S are P

Statement:

All S are P.

The S circle is completely inside the P circle.

      _____________
     /             \
    /      P        \
   |     ______      |
   |    /  S   \     |
   |    \______ /    |
    \             /
     \___________/

Meaning:

Every S belongs to P.

46. Venn Diagram – No S are P

Statement:

No S are P.

The two circles do not overlap.

    _______       _______
   /       \     /       \
  /    S    \   /    P    \
  \         /   \         /
   \_______/     \_______/

Meaning:

There is no common member.

47. Venn Diagram – Some S are P

Statement:

Some S are P.

The two circles overlap.

       _______
      /       \
     /   S   __\____
     \      /  P    \
      \____/         /
           \________/

The overlapping region represents members that belong to both S and P.

48. Venn Diagram – Some S are Not P

Statement:

Some S are not P.

There must be at least one member in the S region outside P.

The important idea is:

S
┌───────────────┐
│   x           │
│       ┌───────┼──────┐
│       │   P   │      │
└───────┴───────┴──────┘

The x represents existence of at least one S that is not P.

49. Using Venn Diagrams to Test Arguments

Consider:

All humans are mortal.

Socrates is a human.

Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

The relationship can be represented as:

Humans ⊂ Mortals

Socrates ∈ Humans

Therefore:

Socrates ∈ Mortals

The conclusion follows.

50. Simple and Multiple Venn Diagrams

Simple Venn Diagram

Usually involves two or three classes.

Example:

Students
Teachers
Researchers

Multiple Venn Diagram

Used when several classes and relationships must be represented simultaneously.

The main purpose is to determine whether the conclusion follows from the premises.

51. Indian Logic

Indian Logic is an important part of the UGC NET Logical Reasoning syllabus.

Indian philosophical traditions developed systematic methods of acquiring knowledge and establishing valid cognition.

One important concept is:

Pramana

52. Meaning of Pramana

Pramana means a means or source of valid knowledge.

Different Indian philosophical schools recognize different numbers and types of Pramanas.

The syllabus specifically includes six:

  1. Pratyaksha
  2. Anumana
  3. Upamana
  4. Shabda
  5. Arthapatti
  6. Anupalabdhi

53. Six Pramanas

Pramana Simple meaning
Pratyaksha Perception
Anumana Inference
Upamana Comparison
Shabda Verbal testimony
Arthapatti Implication / Postulation
Anupalabdhi Non-apprehension

54. Pratyaksha – Perception

Pratyaksha means knowledge obtained through perception.

In simple terms:

Knowledge gained through direct observation or experience.

Example

You see smoke.

The visual perception of smoke is an example of perceptual knowledge.

Another simple example:

You see a tree in front of you.

55. Anumana – Inference

Anumana means inference.

It is knowledge obtained by reasoning from something observed to something not directly observed.

Famous example

There is fire on the hill.

Why?

Because there is smoke.

The reasoning is:

Smoke → Fire

More carefully:

Wherever there is smoke, there is fire.

There is smoke on the hill.

Therefore:

There is fire on the hill.

56. Upamana – Comparison

Upamana means comparison or analogy.

Knowledge is obtained by comparing something with something already known.

Example

Suppose someone tells you:

A gavaya is an animal similar to a cow.

Later you see an unfamiliar animal that resembles a cow and recognize it as a gavaya.

The knowledge is based on comparison.

Key Point

Upamana = Comparison

57. Shabda – Verbal Testimony

Shabda means verbal testimony or reliable verbal instruction.

Knowledge is obtained from a reliable source.

Example

A qualified teacher tells a student:

The Earth revolves around the Sun.

The student obtains knowledge through verbal testimony.

Key Point

Shabda = Verbal testimony

58. Arthapatti – Implication / Postulation

Arthapatti refers to postulation or implication used to explain facts that otherwise cannot be adequately explained.

Simple example

Suppose:

Ravi is usually seen fasting during the day.

But:

Ravi is gaining weight.

A possible postulation is:

Ravi must be eating at night.

The second fact is used to explain the first set of observations.

Key Point

Arthapatti = Postulation / Implication

59. Anupalabdhi – Non-Apprehension

Anupalabdhi means knowledge of the absence of something through non-perception.

Example

You look at a table and do not see a book on it.

Under the relevant conditions, you know:

There is no book on the table.

Key Point

Anupalabdhi = Knowledge of absence through non-apprehension

61. Structure of Anumana

Anumana is inference.

A classical Nyaya inference is commonly presented using five members:

  1. Pratijna
  2. Hetu
  3. Udaharana
  4. Upanaya
  5. Nigamana

62. Pratijna

Pratijna is the proposition or statement to be established.

Example:

The hill has fire.

This is the claim.

63. Hetu

Hetu is the reason or middle term.

Example:

Because there is smoke on the hill.

Here:

Smoke = Hetu

64. Udaharana

Udaharana provides an example illustrating the universal relationship.

Example:

Wherever there is smoke, there is fire, as in a kitchen.

This establishes the relationship between smoke and fire.

65. Upanaya

Upanaya applies the general relationship to the particular case.

Example:

The hill has smoke of that kind.

66. Nigamana

Nigamana is the final conclusion.

Example:

Therefore, the hill has fire.

67. Five Members of Anumana

Member Meaning Example
Pratijna Proposition Hill has fire
Hetu Reason Because of smoke
Udaharana Example Smoke and fire in kitchen
Upanaya Application Hill has such smoke
Nigamana Conclusion Therefore hill has fire

Memory

P → H → U → U → N

Pratijna
Hetu
Udaharana
Upanaya
Nigamana

68. Important Terms in Anumana

Some important terms are:

  • Paksha
  • Sadhya
  • Hetu
  • Vyapti
  • Sapaksha
  • Vipaksha

69. Paksha

Paksha is the subject or locus about which the inference is made.

Example:

Hill

In:

The hill has fire because it has smoke.

The hill is the Paksha.

70. Sadhya

Sadhya is what is to be established.

Example:

Fire

The inference tries to establish that fire exists on the hill.

Therefore:

Sadhya = Fire

71. Hetu

Hetu is the reason used to establish the Sadhya.

Example:

Smoke

Therefore:

Hetu = Smoke

72. Vyapti

Vyapti means an invariable or universal relation between the Hetu and Sadhya.

In the famous example:

Smoke → Fire

The required relationship is:

Wherever there is smoke, there is fire.

This relationship is called Vyapti.

Easy meaning

Vyapti = Invariable relation

73. Sapaksha

Sapaksha refers to cases where the Sadhya is present.

Example:

If fire is the Sadhya, places where fire is present can serve as Sapaksha examples.

A kitchen with fire and smoke can be an example.

74. Vipaksha

Vipaksha refers to cases where the Sadhya is absent.

If fire is the Sadhya, a place where there is no fire can be considered a Vipaksha case.

The purpose is to examine whether the Hetu is absent where the Sadhya is absent.

75. Types of Anumana

Inference can be classified in different ways in Indian logic.

Important classifications include:

1. Svarthanumana

Inference for oneself.

2. Pararthanumana

Inference presented for the understanding of another person.

76. Svarthanumana

Svarthanumana means inference for oneself.

The person internally reasons from the evidence to the conclusion.

Example:

You see smoke coming from a distant hill and infer:

There is fire on the hill.

77. Pararthanumana

Pararthanumana means inference presented to establish a conclusion for another person.

It is commonly expressed through the five-member structure:

Pratijna
↓
Hetu
↓
Udaharana
↓
Upanaya
↓
Nigamana

78. Hetvabhasa

Hetvabhasa means a fallacy of inference.

The word can be understood as:

Hetu = reason
Abhasa = appearance

So Hetvabhasa refers to a reason that appears to be valid but is actually defective.

79. Common Hetvabhasas

Important Hetvabhasas include:

  1. Savyabhichara
  2. Viruddha
  3. Satpratipaksha
  4. Asiddha
  5. Badhita

80. Savyabhichara

Savyabhichara is an irregular or inconclusive reason.

The Hetu does not maintain the required invariable relationship with the Sadhya.

Simple idea

Reason is not consistently connected with conclusion.

81. Viruddha

Viruddha means contradictory reason.

The stated reason actually establishes the opposite of what the argument intends to establish.

Simple idea

The reason supports the opposite conclusion.

82. Satpratipaksha

Satpratipaksha occurs when another valid reason establishes a conclusion that contradicts the original conclusion.

Simple idea

One reason supports conclusion A.

Another valid reason supports conclusion not-A.

The original inference is therefore challenged.

83. Asiddha

Asiddha means an unestablished reason.

The Hetu itself is not properly established.

Simple idea

The reason being used has not been established.

84. Badhita

Badhita occurs when the proposed conclusion is contradicted by stronger or more direct evidence.

Simple idea

The conclusion is contradicted by stronger evidence.

93. Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 – Confusing Deduction and Induction

Remember:

Deduction → General to Specific

Induction → Specific to General

Mistake 2 – Confusing A and E

A → All S are P

E → No S are P

Mistake 3 – Confusing I and O

I → Some S are P

O → Some S are not P

Mistake 4 – Confusing Connotation and Denotation

Denotation → Direct/literal meaning

Connotation → Associated meaning

Mistake 5 – Confusing Hetu and Sadhya

Hetu → Reason

Sadhya → What we want to establish

Mistake 6 – Confusing Vyapti and Hetu

Hetu → Particular reason

Vyapti → Invariable relationship
